1The map

A robot policy \(\pi(a \mid o)\) maps observations (images, joint angles, touch, language) to actions (joint targets, end-effector deltas, torques). Robot learning methods differ mainly in where the supervision comes from.

ParadigmSupervisionStrengthWeakness
Classical controlA model + a cost you writePrecise, safe, interpretableNeeds accurate models; hard with contact and perception
Imitation learningExpert demonstrationsSimple, stable, works on real robots todayBounded by data quality and coverage; compounding errors
Reinforcement learningReward signalCan exceed demonstrators; discovers new behaviorSample hungry; reward design; unsafe exploration
World modelsLearned dynamicsPlanning, data efficiency, "imagination"Model errors get exploited
Foundation modelsInternet + large robot datasetsGeneralization, language conditioningData and compute cost; slow inference

2Problem setup

An MDP is \((\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, r, \gamma, \rho_0)\). The robot usually sees only an observation \(o_t\) (a POMDP), so policies condition on a short history \(o_{t-H:t}\). The goal is to maximize expected discounted return:

$$J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{T} \gamma^t r(s_t, a_t)\Big].$$

Action spaces matter more than you'd think

Joint position targets

Tracked by a PD controller. The most common choice for hands and humanoids.

End-effector pose

Absolute or delta, with IK or operational-space control underneath. Transfers more easily across arms.

Relative actions

Expressed with respect to the current pose (as in TRI's LBM). Generalize better across scene positions.

Torques

The most expressive and the hardest to learn. Need high control rates.

Predicting action chunks \(a_{t:t+K}\) instead of single actions is now standard for imitation learning (see §6). For rotations, avoid Euler-angle outputs. The continuous 6D representation (two columns of the rotation matrix) is a safe default.

3Control basics

Kinematics

Forward kinematics \(x = f(q)\) maps joint angles to the end-effector pose. The Jacobian \(J(q) = \partial f / \partial q\) relates the two velocities, \(\dot x = J \dot q\). Differential IK solves for joint velocities with damped least squares:

$$\dot q = J^\top (J J^\top + \lambda^2 I)^{-1} \dot x_{\text{des}}.$$

Retargeting a human hand to a robot hand is an IK-like optimization: match keypoint or fingertip vectors, usually with collision and smoothness terms.

PD / PID control

$$\tau = K_p (q_{\text{des}} - q) + K_d(\dot q_{\text{des}} - \dot q) \;[+\, K_i \textstyle\int e\,dt].$$

A learned policy usually outputs \(q_{\text{des}}\) at 10–50 Hz, while the PD loop runs at 100–1000 Hz. The gains set how compliant the robot is: low gains are safer for contact but track worse.

InteractivePD step response of a unit mass

The damping ratio \(\zeta = K_d / (2\sqrt{K_p\, m})\) decides the behavior. \(\zeta < 1\) overshoots and rings, \(\zeta = 1\) is critically damped, and \(\zeta > 1\) is sluggish.

Impedance and operational-space control

To make the end effector behave like a spring-damper in task space:

$$\tau = J^\top\big(K (x_{\text{des}} - x) - D \dot x\big) + g(q).$$

Operational-space control (Khatib) also accounts for the task-space inertia \(\Lambda = (J M^{-1} J^\top)^{-1}\), so forces are decoupled. Compliance is what makes contact-rich tasks like insertion and wiping forgiving.

LQR and MPC

For linear dynamics \(x_{t+1} = A x_t + B u_t\) and quadratic cost \(\sum x^\top Q x + u^\top R u\), the optimal policy is linear, \(u = -Kx\). The gain \(K\) comes from the Riccati equation. iLQR/DDP extend this to nonlinear systems by linearizing around a trajectory. MPC re-solves a finite-horizon problem at every step, executes the first action, and replans:

$$a^*_{t:t+H} = \arg\min_{a_{t:t+H}} \sum_{k=0}^{H} c(x_{t+k}, a_{t+k}) \quad \text{s.t. } x_{k+1} = f(x_k, a_k).$$

Sampling-based MPC (CEM, MPPI) needs no gradients: sample action sequences, roll them out, and reweight. MPPI's update is \(a \leftarrow \sum_i w_i a^{(i)}\) with \(w_i \propto \exp(-C^{(i)}/\lambda)\). TD-MPC runs MPPI over a learned latent model and bootstraps with a learned Q-function.

Learned policies and classical control are complements. Learning handles perception and the high-level choice of action. Control handles stability, safety limits, and high-rate tracking.

4Imitation learning

Behavior cloning

BC is supervised learning on demonstrations \(\mathcal{D} = \{(o, a)\}\):

$$\min_\theta \; \mathbb{E}_{(o,a)\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[-\log \pi_\theta(a \mid o)\big].$$

Compounding error. For a horizon \(T\) and per-step error \(\epsilon\), naive BC can incur \(O(\epsilon T^2)\) cost, versus \(O(\epsilon T)\) for interactive methods like DAgger, which query the expert on the learner's own states. In practice people mitigate it with diverse data, noise injection during collection (DART), action chunking, and recovery demonstrations.

Why MSE regression fails: multimodality

Fixes, roughly in historical order:

  • Gaussian mixture heads
  • Discretized actions with a categorical output (RT-1, binning)
  • Latent-variable models (CVAE, as in ACT)
  • Energy-based models (Implicit BC)
  • VQ tokens (VQ-BeT) and frequency-space tokens (FAST)
  • Diffusion and flow-matching policies, today's default

The quality, consistency, and coverage of the data usually matter more than the architecture. Demonstration sources include teleoperation (VR, leader–follower arms like ALOHA/GELLO, gloves), handheld grippers (UMI), human videos and mocap (DexCap), and simulation.

5Generative models primer

Diffusion (DDPM)

The forward process gradually adds noise, \(x_t = \alpha_t x_0 + \sigma_t \epsilon\) with \(\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\). A network learns to predict the noise:

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{x_0, \epsilon, t}\, \|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\|^2.$$

Predicting noise is equivalent to learning the score, \(\nabla_x \log p_t(x) \approx -\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)/\sigma_t\). DDPM samples with a stochastic reverse process; DDIM is deterministic and needs fewer steps.

SDE / ODE view

The reverse SDE \(dx = [f - g^2 \nabla_x \log p_t(x)]dt + g\, d\bar w\) shares its marginals with the probability-flow ODE \(dx = [f - \tfrac12 g^2 \nabla \log p_t]dt\). The Fokker–Planck equation describes how the density evolves under either. Higher-order ODE solvers (DPM-Solver) cut sampling to 10–20 steps. Langevin dynamics, \(x \leftarrow x + \eta \nabla \log p(x) + \sqrt{2\eta}\,\xi\), samples from a fixed distribution using only its score.

Flow matching and stochastic interpolants

Pick an interpolation path between noise \(x_0\) and data \(x_1\), for example the straight line \(x_t = (1-t)x_0 + t x_1\), and regress the velocity along it:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}} = \mathbb{E}_{t, x_0, x_1}\,\|v_\theta(x_t, t) - (x_1 - x_0)\|^2.$$

Sampling integrates \(\dot x = v_\theta(x, t)\) from \(t=0\) to \(1\). With Gaussian paths, flow matching and diffusion are the same model under a different parameterization and time schedule (diffusionflow.github.io). Stochastic interpolants (Albergo et al.) generalize the framework to arbitrary endpoint pairs.

Guidance

Classifier-free guidance trains one model both with and without the condition \(c\), then extrapolates between the two predictions:

$$\tilde\epsilon = \epsilon_\theta(x_t, \varnothing) + w\,\big(\epsilon_\theta(x_t, c) - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, \varnothing)\big).$$

Autoguidance replaces the unconditional branch with a worse version of the same model, which improves quality without collapsing diversity.

Few-step and one-step generation

  • Consistency models learn \(f(x_t, t) \to x_0\), consistent along each ODE trajectory, so a single step works.
  • Distillation (progressive or adversarial) compresses a many-step teacher into a few-step student.
  • MeanFlow learns the average velocity over an interval \([r, t]\) instead of the instantaneous one, enabling one-step sampling trained from scratch.
  • Shortcut models condition on the step size.

Speed matters for robots: a 10-step policy queried at 10 Hz is often too slow. Few-step generation is a robotics problem, not just an image-generation one.

6Diffusion & flow policies

Diffusion Policy (Chi et al., 2023) treats an action chunk \(A_t = a_{t:t+K}\) as the sample and the observation as the condition:

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}\,\|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(A_t^{(k)}, k, o_{t-H:t})\|^2.$$

At test time, start from Gaussian noise, denoise to a chunk, execute the first \(K_e \le K\) actions, then replan (receding horizon). Common backbones are a 1D temporal U-Net with FiLM conditioning, or a DiT with adaLN. Flow-matching policies (as in \(\pi_0\)) are the same idea trained with the FM loss.

Why action chunking helps

  • Temporal consistency: a chunk commits to one mode, so the robot doesn't jitter between strategies on consecutive steps.
  • Less compounding error: the policy makes fewer decisions per episode.
  • Robustness to pauses and non-Markovian behavior in the demonstrations.
  • Trade-off: reactivity. Long open-loop execution can't respond to disturbances. Recent analysis (arXiv:2507.09061) studies when chunking helps or hurts.

Smoothing between chunks: ACT-style temporal ensembling averages overlapping predictions. RTC treats the next chunk as inpainting conditioned on committed actions. Streaming flow policies integrate the flow along the action trajectory itself and emit actions as they go.

Speed, history, imperfect data

Making it fast

DDIM with fewer steps, consistency policies, one-step distillation, a small action expert on a large VLM, asynchronous inference.

Long context

Long histories often hurt BC through copycat or causal confusion. Fixes include predicting past actions as an auxiliary task, history dropout, and compressed memory.

Imperfect data

Ambient-diffusion-style training uses low-quality data only at high noise levels (coarse structure) and clean data at low noise levels (fine detail).

Understanding

Do diffusion policies really learn multimodal distributions, or mostly memorize a lookup table? How well do they respect kinematic constraints? Both are active analysis topics.

7Reinforcement learning

Value functions

$$V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\Big[\sum_k \gamma^k r_{t+k} \,\Big|\, s_t = s\Big],\quad Q^\pi(s,a) = r + \gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{s'}[V^\pi(s')],\quad A^\pi = Q^\pi - V^\pi.$$

The Bellman optimality equation is \(Q^*(s,a) = r + \gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{s'}\max_{a'} Q^*(s',a')\).

Policy gradient

$$\nabla_\theta J = \mathbb{E}_{s \sim d^\pi_\gamma,\, a \sim \pi_\theta}\big[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a\mid s)\, A^\pi(s,a)\big].$$

REINFORCE uses the Monte-Carlo return in place of \(A\). Subtracting a baseline \(V(s)\) reduces variance without adding bias. GAE interpolates between low-variance TD estimates and unbiased Monte-Carlo estimates:

$$\hat A_t = \sum_{l\ge0} (\gamma\lambda)^l \delta_{t+l},\qquad \delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t).$$

"Policy gradient is not a gradient." The theorem requires states drawn from the discounted state distribution \(d^\pi_\gamma(s) \propto \sum_t \gamma^t \Pr(s_t = s)\). Practical implementations sample states uniformly from rollouts, dropping the \(\gamma^t\) factor. The resulting update is not the gradient of any objective (Thomas 2014; Nota & Thomas 2020, arXiv:1906.07073). It works well in practice anyway.

PPO

PPO takes several gradient steps on the same batch while keeping the policy close to the one that collected the data:

$$\mathcal{L}^{\text{clip}} = \mathbb{E}\big[\min(\rho_t \hat A_t,\; \text{clip}(\rho_t, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\hat A_t)\big],\quad \rho_t = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)}.$$
InteractiveThe PPO clipped objective for one sample

PPO is on-policy, simple, and scales with massively parallel simulation (Isaac Lab, MuJoCo Playground). It is the workhorse for sim-to-real locomotion and in-hand manipulation.

The Q-learning family

  • DQN: a neural Q-function with replay and a target network. Rainbow combines Double DQN, dueling heads, prioritized replay, n-step returns, distributional Q (C51), and noisy nets.
  • DDPG / TD3: continuous actions via a deterministic actor that maximizes Q. TD3 adds clipped double-Q and target smoothing.
  • SAC: maximum-entropy RL, \(J = \mathbb{E}\sum_t [r_t + \alpha \mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot\mid s_t))]\), with twin Q-networks and auto-tuned \(\alpha\). The off-policy default for real-robot learning.

Tricks that matter: a high update-to-data ratio needs regularization to stay stable. Options include ensembles (REDQ), dropout plus LayerNorm (DroQ), periodic resets, and LayerNorm in critics. Other important choices are observation normalization, n-step returns, and symmetric sampling from demos (RLPD). BRO and SimBa scale critics with these tricks.

8Offline RL

Offline RL learns from a fixed dataset without environment interaction. The core problem: \(\max_{a'} Q(s',a')\) queries actions the data never contains, and Q extrapolates optimistically there.

CQL

Pushes Q down on policy actions and up on data actions: \(\alpha(\mathbb{E}_{\pi}Q - \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}Q)\) plus the Bellman loss.

IQL

Never queries out-of-distribution actions. Fits \(V\) by expectile regression on \(Q\), then extracts a policy with advantage-weighted regression.

Cal-QL

Calibrates CQL's conservatism so online fine-tuning doesn't start with a performance dip.

RLPD

Offline-to-online: sample 50/50 from offline data and online replay, with a high UTD ratio. A strong, simple baseline.

9Model-based RL

  • Dreamer (V1–V3): learns a recurrent state-space model and trains the actor-critic entirely in imagined latent rollouts.
  • TD-MPC: TOLD learns a latent encoder, latent dynamics, reward, and Q with latent-consistency and TD losses but no reconstruction. At inference it runs MPPI over a short horizon and bootstraps with Q for the long horizon.
  • TD-MPC2: adds learnable task embeddings, discrete regression in a log-transformed space for reward and value, a max-entropy policy prior, and SimNorm latent normalization. A single agent scales to 80+ tasks.
  • MBPO: short model rollouts branched from real states, used to augment SAC's replay buffer.

Keep model rollouts short, or plan in a latent space trained for prediction of value rather than pixels. Long open-loop model rollouts accumulate error, and the policy learns to exploit it.

10RL × generative policies

Generative policies are expressive, but their likelihood \(\pi(a\mid s)\) is intractable, which complicates policy gradients. The main approaches:

11Sim-to-real

  • Domain randomization: randomize physics, latency, observation noise, and visuals.
  • Adaptation (RMA): encode the history into a latent estimate of the environment parameters, so the policy adapts online.
  • System identification / real-to-sim: fit simulator parameters or reconstruct scenes (Gaussian splatting, meshes).

Actuator modeling, control latency, and PD gains cause most sim-to-real failures, more often than visuals do.

12Dexterous manipulation

Framework 1: learn from humans

Estimate hand pose (MANO) from video, mocap, or VR; retarget to the robot hand by optimizing keypoint vectors with collision and smoothness terms; train IL, optionally followed by RL. DexMV, DIME, Holo-Dex, VideoDex, AnyTeleop, DexCap, CyberDemo.

Framework 2: learn in simulation

RL with privileged state, distilled to a vision/touch student and transferred with domain randomization. In-hand rotation (Qi et al.), twisting lids, DexPoint, DextrAH-G, Sequential Dexterity.

Recurring design choices:

  • Object-centric inputs, such as fingertip-to-object distances and object pose in the wrist frame
  • Low-dimensional action manifolds (PCA "eigengrasps")
  • Contact-based rewards
  • Tactile sensing for the occlusions that vision always suffers in-hand
  • A pre-grasp → grasp → post-grasp decomposition

13Foundation models / VLAs

The action head is either autoregressive tokens (RT-2, OpenVLA, FAST) or a diffusion/flow action expert (\(\pi_0\), GR00T, TRI LBM).

Example recipe: TRI Large Behavior Model

DiT with adaLN; CLIP ViT features for image and language; DDIM training; relative actions; 2-step observation history; 15-step chunks of 20-D actions. Data: ~1,700 hours of internal real, sim, and UMI data plus Open-X. Per-dataset normalization; crop and color-jitter augmentation; idle initial frames filtered out.

  • Adapting a VLA: full fine-tuning, LoRA, ControlNet-style side branches (ControlVLA), or RL fine-tuning (§10).
  • Inference speed: action-expert distillation, fewer denoising steps, KV caching, RTC and asynchronous execution.
  • VLMs without end-to-end training: have the VLM output keypoints, affordances, or plans, and let low-level policies execute them.

14World models & video

  • Diffusion Forcing (v1, v2): per-frame independent noise; masking becomes a form of noise. Gives stable long rollouts and guidance-based planning.
  • Unified Video Action model (UVA): a joint video–action latent with separate diffusion heads. Masking determines whether it acts as a policy, forward or inverse dynamics model, or video generator.
  • V-JEPA 2: self-supervised latent prediction (no pixel reconstruction) on over 1M hours of video, with a resolution and length curriculum. An action-conditioned variant post-trained on a little robot data plans with CEM in latent space.
  • Video models as data engines: DreamGen generates synthetic robot videos, labels them with inverse dynamics, and trains on the pseudo-actions.
  • Learned simulators for evaluation: roll out a policy in an action-conditioned video model to rank checkpoints cheaply.

15Agentic robotics

A VLA maps one instruction to motor commands for a few seconds. An agentic system wraps a reasoning model around such skills and runs it in a loop: look at the scene, plan, call a tool (a VLA skill, a perception model, a motion planner, code, search, a human), check what happened, and re-plan. It is the robotics version of LLM agents that use tools, and it targets long-horizon tasks, recovery from failure, and instructions that need world knowledge.

How the planner talks to the robot

The main design choice is the interface between the slow reasoning model and the fast controller:

  • Language subtasks (SayCan, Hi Robot, \(\pi_{0.5}\)'s high-level step, Gemini Robotics 1.5): the planner says "pick up the blue umbrella" and a language-conditioned VLA executes it. Simple and general; limited by what the low-level policy can do.
  • Code (Code as Policies, ProgPrompt): the planner writes a program over perception and motion APIs. Precise loops, conditions and arithmetic; brittle when the API doesn't fit the task.
  • Spatial intermediates (VoxPoser, MOKA, ReKep): the planner outputs keypoints, affordance maps or constraints, and an optimizer or planner turns them into motion. Grounded in geometry, but mostly for kinematic tasks.
  • One model, two speeds (GR00T N1, Figure's Helix, \(\pi_{0.5}\)): a single network with a slow VLM part and a fast action part, trained end to end. Less modular, but no hand-designed interface.

Closing the loop

  • Feedback: after each step, feed back success detection, scene descriptions or human corrections, then re-plan (Inner Monologue). Without this an open-loop plan can't recover from a single failed grasp.
  • Thinking before acting: newer embodied-reasoning models (Gemini Robotics-ER, Cosmos-Reason) are trained to reason about space, physics and progress before choosing the next step.
  • Memory: long tasks need a record of what was done and where things are, such as a log of past steps or a spatial index of objects seen (ReMEmbR).
  • Asking for help: calibrated uncertainty tells the agent when to ask a person instead of guessing (KnowNo).

Agents that do robot learning

Agents also automate parts of the research loop itself:

  • Rewards: an LLM writes and iterates on reward code from training feedback (Eureka, Text2Reward), including domain randomization ranges for sim-to-real (DrEureka).
  • Tasks and environments: generate simulation scenes and tasks for training (RoboGen, GenSim).
  • Data collection: a VLM proposes tasks across a robot fleet, filters them for safety and feasibility, and scores the outcomes (AutoRT, SOAR), producing data for self-improvement.

Where it breaks

Most failures still come from the low-level skill, not the plan. A planner can't recover from a skill that doesn't exist. Calling a large model each step adds seconds of latency. Errors compound over long horizons, and a confident wrong success check is worse than none. Physical actions can't be undone the way a code edit can, so unsafe-action filters, safety rules for the planner (ASIMOV) and human confirmation for irreversible steps matter more than in software agents. Evaluation is hard too: report success per subtask and over the whole task, plus how often the agent recovers from failures.

16Practical checklist

  1. Get the controller and action space right first: control rate, gains, delta vs. absolute, the frame actions are expressed in.
  2. Normalize actions and proprioception, per dataset when mixing datasets.
  3. Check the data: replay demos open-loop on the robot, look for idle segments and inconsistent strategies, verify timestamp alignment.
  4. Start from a strong baseline: Diffusion Policy or ACT, a pretrained ResNet or ViT, crop augmentation, 1–2 frames of history.
  5. Evaluate honestly: fixed initial conditions, 20+ trials per setting, variance reported, blind A/B tests between checkpoints.
  6. Validation loss poorly predicts closed-loop success in BC. Trust rollouts.
  7. For RL, test the reward on scripted behaviors before training, and watch rollouts early and often.
