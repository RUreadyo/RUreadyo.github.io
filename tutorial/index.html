Robot Learning Primer

A short tour of policy learning for robots: control, imitation learning, generative policies, reinforcement learning, and where large models fit in. Each section gives the core equations, the intuition behind them, and the papers to read next.

1. The map

A robot policy \(\pi(a \mid o)\) maps observations (images, joint angles, touch, language) to actions (joint targets, end-effector deltas, torques). Most robot learning methods differ in where the supervision comes from:

ParadigmSupervisionStrengthWeakness
Classical controlA model + a cost you writePrecise, safe, interpretableNeeds accurate models; hard with contact and perception
Imitation learning (IL)Expert demonstrationsSimple and stable; works on real robots todayBounded by data quality and coverage; compounding errors
Reinforcement learning (RL)Reward signalCan exceed demonstrators; discovers new behaviorSample hungry; reward design; unsafe exploration
Model-based / world modelsLearned dynamicsPlanning, data efficiency, "imagination"Model errors get exploited
Foundation models (VLA)Internet + large robot datasetsGeneralization, language conditioningData and compute cost; slow inference

The current practical recipe for manipulation combines them: pretrain a large generative policy on demonstrations, then adapt it with fine-tuning or RL, and run it with a low-level controller underneath.

2. Problem setup

An MDP is \((\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, r, \gamma, \rho_0)\). The robot usually sees only an observation \(o_t\) (a POMDP), so policies condition on a short history \(o_{t-H:t}\). The goal is to maximize expected discounted return:

$$J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{T} \gamma^t r(s_t, a_t)\Big].$$

Action spaces matter more than you'd think

  • Joint position targets are tracked by a PD controller. This is the most common choice for hands and humanoids.
  • End-effector pose (absolute or delta) needs IK or operational-space control underneath. It transfers more easily across arms.
  • Relative/delta actions are expressed with respect to the current pose (as in TRI's LBM). They generalize better across scene positions.
  • Torques are the most expressive and the hardest to learn. They also need high control rates.
  • Action chunks predict \(a_{t:t+K}\) at once. See §6.

Rotations: avoid Euler angles as network outputs. The continuous 6D representation (two columns of the rotation matrix) is a safe default.

3. Control basics

Kinematics

Forward kinematics \(x = f(q)\) maps joint angles to the end-effector pose. The Jacobian \(J(q) = \partial f / \partial q\) relates the two velocities, \(\dot x = J \dot q\). Differential IK solves for joint velocities with damped least squares:

$$\dot q = J^\top (J J^\top + \lambda^2 I)^{-1} \dot x_{\text{des}}.$$

Retargeting a human hand to a robot hand is an IK-like optimization. It matches keypoint or fingertip vectors, often with collision and smoothness terms.

PD / PID control

$$\tau = K_p (q_{\text{des}} - q) + K_d(\dot q_{\text{des}} - \dot q) \;[+\, K_i \textstyle\int e\,dt].$$

A learned policy usually outputs \(q_{\text{des}}\) at 10–50 Hz, while the PD loop runs at 100–1000 Hz. The gains set how compliant the robot is. Low gains are safer for contact but track worse.

Impedance and operational-space control

To make the end effector behave like a spring-damper in task space:

$$\tau = J^\top\big(K (x_{\text{des}} - x) - D \dot x\big) + g(q).$$

Operational-space control (Khatib) also accounts for the task-space inertia \(\Lambda = (J M^{-1} J^\top)^{-1}\), so forces are decoupled. Compliance is what makes contact-rich tasks like insertion and wiping forgiving.

LQR

For linear dynamics \(x_{t+1} = A x_t + B u_t\) and quadratic cost \(\sum x^\top Q x + u^\top R u\), the optimal policy is linear, \(u = -Kx\). The gain \(K\) comes from the Riccati equation. iLQR/DDP extend this to nonlinear systems by linearizing around a trajectory.

Model predictive control (MPC)

At every step, solve a finite-horizon problem, execute the first action, then replan:

$$a^*_{t:t+H} = \arg\min_{a_{t:t+H}} \sum_{k=0}^{H} c(x_{t+k}, a_{t+k}) \quad \text{s.t. } x_{k+1} = f(x_k, a_k).$$

Sampling-based MPC (CEM, MPPI) needs no gradients. Sample action sequences, roll them out, and reweight. MPPI's update is \(a \leftarrow \sum_i w_i a^{(i)}\) with \(w_i \propto \exp(-\tfrac{1}{\lambda} C^{(i)})\). TD-MPC combines MPPI over a learned latent model with a learned terminal Q-function.

Learned policies and classical control are complements. Learning handles perception and the high-level action choice. Control handles stability, safety limits, and high-rate tracking.

4. Imitation learning

Behavior cloning (BC)

BC is supervised learning on demonstrations \(\mathcal{D} = \{(o, a)\}\):

$$\min_\theta \; \mathbb{E}_{(o,a)\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[-\log \pi_\theta(a \mid o)\big].$$

Compounding error. Small mistakes take the robot to states the demonstrations never covered, and the errors grow. For a horizon \(T\) and per-step error \(\epsilon\), naive BC can incur \(O(\epsilon T^2)\) cost, versus \(O(\epsilon T)\) for interactive methods like DAgger, which query the expert on the learner's own states. In practice people mitigate this with more diverse data, noise injection during collection (DART), action chunking, and closed-loop recovery data.

Why MSE regression fails: multimodality

If demonstrators sometimes go left around an obstacle and sometimes right, an L2-regressed policy averages the two and goes straight into the obstacle. Fixes, roughly in historical order:

  • Gaussian mixture heads (GMM)
  • Discretized actions with a categorical output (RT-1, binning)
  • Latent-variable models (CVAE, as in ACT)
  • Energy-based models (Implicit BC)
  • VQ tokens (VQ-BeT) and frequency-space tokens (FAST)
  • Diffusion and flow-matching policies, today's default (§6)

Data

Demonstration sources include teleoperation (VR, leader-follower arms like ALOHA/GELLO, gloves), handheld grippers (UMI), human videos and mocap (DexCap), and simulation. The quality, consistency, and coverage of the data usually matter more than the architecture.

5. Generative models primer

Diffusion (DDPM)

The forward process gradually adds noise: \(x_t = \alpha_t x_0 + \sigma_t \epsilon\) with \(\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\). A network learns to predict the noise:

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{x_0, \epsilon, t}\, \|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\|^2.$$

This is equivalent to learning the score, \(\nabla_x \log p_t(x) \approx -\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)/\sigma_t\). Sampling runs the reverse process. DDPM uses a stochastic sampler; DDIM is deterministic and needs fewer steps.

SDE / ODE view

The continuous-time reverse SDE is \(dx = [f(x,t) - g(t)^2 \nabla_x \log p_t(x)]dt + g(t) d\bar w\). It shares its marginals with the probability-flow ODE \(dx = [f - \tfrac12 g^2 \nabla \log p_t]dt\). The Fokker–Planck equation describes how the density evolves under either. Higher-order ODE solvers (DPM-Solver) cut sampling to 10–20 steps. Langevin dynamics, \(x \leftarrow x + \eta \nabla \log p(x) + \sqrt{2\eta}\,\xi\), samples from a fixed distribution using only its score.

Flow matching / stochastic interpolants

Pick an interpolation path between noise \(x_0\) and data \(x_1\), for example the straight line \(x_t = (1-t)x_0 + t x_1\). Regress the velocity along it:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}} = \mathbb{E}_{t, x_0, x_1}\,\|v_\theta(x_t, t) - (x_1 - x_0)\|^2.$$

Sampling integrates \(\dot x = v_\theta(x, t)\) from \(t=0\) to \(1\). With Gaussian paths, flow matching and diffusion are the same model under a different parameterization and time schedule. Stochastic interpolants (Albergo et al.) generalize the framework to arbitrary endpoint pairs. The diffusionflow.github.io post covers the equivalence well.

Guidance

Classifier-free guidance (CFG) trains one model both with and without the condition \(c\), then extrapolates between the two predictions:

$$\tilde\epsilon = \epsilon_\theta(x_t, \varnothing) + w\,\big(\epsilon_\theta(x_t, c) - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, \varnothing)\big).$$

Autoguidance replaces the unconditional branch with a worse version of the same model, which improves quality without collapsing diversity.

Few-step and one-step generation

  • Consistency models learn \(f(x_t, t) \to x_0\), consistent along each ODE trajectory, so a single step works.
  • Progressive or adversarial distillation compresses a many-step teacher into a few-step student.
  • MeanFlow learns the average velocity over an interval \([r, t]\) instead of the instantaneous one, which enables one-step sampling trained from scratch.
  • Shortcut models condition on the step size.

This matters for robots: a 10-step policy running at 10 Hz is often too slow.

6. Diffusion and flow policies

Diffusion Policy (Chi et al., 2023) treats an action chunk \(A_t = a_{t:t+K}\) as the sample and the observation as the condition:

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}\,\|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(A_t^{(k)}, k, o_{t-H:t})\|^2.$$

At test time, start from Gaussian noise, denoise to a chunk, execute the first \(K_e \le K\) actions, then replan (receding horizon). Common backbones are a 1D temporal U-Net with FiLM conditioning, or a transformer/DiT with adaLN. Flow-matching policies (as in \(\pi_0\)) are the same idea trained with the FM loss.

Why action chunking helps

  • Temporal consistency. A chunk commits to one mode, so the robot doesn't jitter between left and right on consecutive steps.
  • Less compounding error. The policy makes fewer decisions per episode.
  • Robustness to non-Markovian or pausing demonstrators.
  • The trade-off is reactivity. Long open-loop execution can't respond to disturbances. Recent analysis (e.g. arXiv:2507.09061) studies when chunking helps or hurts from a control-theoretic angle.

Smooth transitions between chunks: ACT-style temporal ensembling averages overlapping predictions. Real-Time Chunking (RTC) treats the next chunk as inpainting conditioned on actions already committed, so the robot can keep executing while the next chunk is being computed. Streaming flow policies integrate the flow along the action trajectory itself and emit actions as they go.

Making it fast

Options include DDIM with fewer steps, consistency policies, one-step distillation, variable-noise schedules, smaller action experts attached to a large VLM backbone, and asynchronous inference.

History and long context

Long observation histories often hurt BC through copycat or causal confusion: the policy learns to copy its previous action instead of reacting to the scene. Approaches that make long context work include predicting past actions as an auxiliary task, history dropout, and compressed memory.

Learning from imperfect data

Ambient-diffusion-style training uses low-quality or out-of-domain demonstrations only at high noise levels, where coarse structure is learned, and keeps clean data for low noise levels, where fine detail is learned.

7. Reinforcement learning

Value functions

$$V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\Big[\sum_k \gamma^k r_{t+k} \,\Big|\, s_t = s\Big],\qquad Q^\pi(s,a) = r(s,a) + \gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{s'}[V^\pi(s')],\qquad A^\pi = Q^\pi - V^\pi.$$

The Bellman optimality equation is \(Q^*(s,a) = r + \gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{s'}\max_{a'} Q^*(s',a')\).

Policy gradient

$$\nabla_\theta J = \mathbb{E}_{s \sim d^\pi_\gamma,\, a \sim \pi_\theta}\big[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a\mid s)\, A^\pi(s,a)\big].$$

REINFORCE uses the Monte-Carlo return in place of \(A\). Subtracting a baseline \(V(s)\) reduces variance without adding bias. GAE interpolates between low-variance TD estimates and unbiased Monte-Carlo estimates:

$$\hat A_t = \sum_{l\ge0} (\gamma\lambda)^l \delta_{t+l},\qquad \delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t).$$
Subtlety: "policy gradient is not a gradient." The theorem requires states drawn from the discounted state distribution \(d^\pi_\gamma(s) \propto \sum_t \gamma^t \Pr(s_t = s)\). Practical implementations sample states uniformly from rollouts, i.e., they drop the \(\gamma^t\) factor. The resulting update is not the gradient of any objective (Thomas 2014; Nota & Thomas 2020, arXiv:1906.07073). It works well in practice anyway, but keep the distinction in mind when doing theory.

PPO

PPO takes multiple gradient steps on the same batch while preventing the policy from moving too far from the one that collected the data:

$$\mathcal{L}^{\text{clip}} = \mathbb{E}\big[\min(\rho_t \hat A_t,\; \text{clip}(\rho_t, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\hat A_t)\big],\quad \rho_t = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)}.$$

PPO is on-policy, simple, and scales with massively parallel simulation (Isaac Gym/Lab, MuJoCo Playground). It is the workhorse for sim-to-real locomotion and in-hand manipulation.

Q-learning family

  • DQN: neural Q-function with a replay buffer and a target network. The improvements combined in Rainbow are Double DQN (fixes overestimation), dueling heads, prioritized replay, n-step returns, distributional Q (C51), and noisy nets.
  • DDPG / TD3: continuous actions via a deterministic actor that maximizes Q. TD3 adds clipped double-Q and target smoothing.
  • SAC: maximum-entropy RL. The objective is \(J = \mathbb{E}\sum_t [r_t + \alpha \mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot\mid s_t))]\). It uses a stochastic actor, twin Q-networks, and auto-tuned \(\alpha\). It is the off-policy default for sample-efficient learning on real robots.

Tricks that matter

A high update-to-data (UTD) ratio needs regularization to stay stable. Options include ensembles (REDQ), dropout plus layer norm (DroQ), periodic resets, and LayerNorm in critics. Other important choices are observation normalization, reward scaling, n-step returns, and symmetric sampling from demos (RLPD). Recent work scales critics with these normalization tricks (e.g., BRO, SimBa).

8. Offline RL

Offline RL learns from a fixed dataset without interacting with the environment. The core problem is that \(\max_{a'} Q(s',a')\) queries actions the data never contains, and Q extrapolates optimistically there.

  • CQL pushes Q down on policy actions and up on data actions: \(\alpha\,(\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}Q - \mathbb{E}_{a\sim\mathcal{D}}Q)\) plus the Bellman loss.
  • IQL never queries out-of-distribution actions. It fits \(V\) by expectile regression on \(Q\), which acts as a soft max over in-data actions, then extracts a policy by advantage-weighted regression, \(\exp(\beta A)\log\pi(a\mid s)\).
  • Cal-QL calibrates CQL's conservatism so the transition to online fine-tuning doesn't start with a performance dip.
  • Offline-to-online: RLPD (sample 50/50 from offline data and online replay, with a high UTD ratio) is a strong, simple baseline.

9. Model-based RL

  • Dreamer (V1–V3): learns a recurrent state-space model (RSSM) and trains the actor-critic entirely in imagined latent rollouts.
  • TD-MPC: TOLD learns a latent encoder, latent dynamics, reward, and Q, trained with latent-consistency and TD losses but no reconstruction. At inference it runs MPPI over a short horizon and bootstraps with Q for the long horizon.
  • TD-MPC2 adds learnable task embeddings, discrete regression in a log-transformed space for reward and value, a max-entropy policy prior, and SimNorm latent normalization. A single agent scales across 80+ tasks.
  • MBPO: short model rollouts branched from real states, used to augment SAC's replay buffer.

10. RL × diffusion and flow policies

Generative policies are expressive, but their likelihood \(\pi(a\mid s)\) is intractable, which complicates policy gradients. The main approaches:

11. Sim-to-real

  • Domain randomization: randomize mass, friction, damping, latency, observation noise, and visuals so the real world looks like just another sample.
  • Teacher–student / privileged distillation: train a teacher with RL on privileged simulator state (object pose, contacts), then distill it into a student that uses only deployable sensors (vision, proprioception, touch) via DAgger.
  • Adaptation (RMA): encode the history into a latent estimate of the environment parameters, so the policy adapts online.
  • System identification and real-to-sim: fit simulator parameters, or reconstruct scenes (Gaussian splatting, meshes), to shrink the gap.
  • Watch out for actuator modeling, control latency, and PD gains. These cause most sim-to-real failures, more often than visuals.

12. Dexterous manipulation

Two standard pipelines:

Framework 1: learn from humans. Estimate hand pose (MANO) from video, mocap, or VR; retarget it to the robot hand by optimizing keypoint or fingertip vectors with collision and smoothness terms; train IL, optionally followed by RL fine-tuning. Examples: DexMV, DIME, Holo-Dex, VideoDex, AnyTeleop, DexCap, CyberDemo.
Framework 2: learn in simulation. Train RL with privileged state, distill to a vision/touch student, and transfer with domain randomization. Examples: in-hand rotation (Qi et al.), twisting lids with two hands, DexPoint, DextrAH-G, Sequential Dexterity.

Recurring design choices:

  • Object-centric inputs, such as fingertip-to-object distances and object pose in the wrist frame
  • Low-dimensional action manifolds (PCA "eigengrasps")
  • Contact-based rewards
  • Tactile sensing for the occlusions that vision always suffers in-hand
  • A pre-grasp → grasp → post-grasp decomposition

13. Robot foundation models / VLAs

A typical VLA has a pretrained VLM backbone (vision encoder plus LLM) and an action head. The head can be autoregressive tokens (RT-2, OpenVLA, FAST) or a diffusion/flow "action expert" (\(\pi_0\), GR00T, TRI LBM).

Example recipe: TRI Large Behavior Model. DiT with adaLN; CLIP ViT features for image and language; DDIM training; relative actions; 2-step observation history; 15-step action chunks (20-D actions). Data: ~1,700 hours combining internal real, sim, and UMI data with Open-X. Per-dataset normalization; crop and color-jitter augmentation; idle initial frames filtered out.

Adapting a VLA: full fine-tuning, LoRA, ControlNet-style side branches (ControlVLA), or RL fine-tuning (see §10). Inference speed: action-expert distillation, fewer denoising steps, KV caching, RTC and asynchronous execution. Using VLMs without end-to-end training: have the VLM output keypoints, affordances, or plans, and let low-level policies or controllers execute them.

14. World models and video generation

  • Diffusion Forcing: train with an independent noise level per frame (token). Partial masking becomes a form of noise. This unifies next-token autoregression with full-sequence diffusion, giving stable long rollouts and guidance-based planning.
  • Unified Video Action model (UVA): a joint latent for video and actions with separate diffusion heads. Masking determines whether the model acts as a policy, forward dynamics, inverse dynamics, or video generator.
  • V-JEPA 2: self-supervised latent prediction (no pixel reconstruction) on over 1M hours of video, with a progressive resolution and length curriculum. An action-conditioned variant is post-trained on a small amount of robot data and plans with CEM in latent space.
  • Video models as data engines: DreamGen generates synthetic robot videos, labels them with an inverse-dynamics model, and trains policies on the resulting pseudo-actions.
  • Learned simulators for evaluation: roll out a policy inside an action-conditioned video model to rank checkpoints cheaply.

15. Practical checklist

  1. Get the controller and action space right first: control rate, gains, delta vs. absolute actions, the frame they're expressed in.
  2. Normalize actions and proprioception, per dataset when mixing datasets.
  3. Check the data: replay demos open-loop on the robot, look for idle segments and inconsistent strategies, and check timestamp alignment between cameras and joint states.
  4. Start with a strong baseline: Diffusion Policy or ACT with a pretrained ResNet or ViT, crop augmentation, and 1–2 frames of history.
  5. Evaluate honestly: fixed initial conditions, enough trials (20+ per setting), report variance, and do blind A/B tests between checkpoints.
  6. Validation loss poorly predicts closed-loop success in BC. Rely on rollouts, or simulation proxies.
  7. For RL, check the reward on scripted behaviors before training, and visualize rollouts early and often.
